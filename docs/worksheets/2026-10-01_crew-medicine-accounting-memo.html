Accounting for medicine the crew carries

A change to when Redwing expenses animal health, not to where
JFR Ranch Co. Ltd.
1 October 2026
To Jayci and Brenda — from John

Now that we have a physical medicine count, there is a decision in front of us that was not worth making before. It is small to implement and it changes the animal health numbers more than it looks like it should. I want you both to see the reasoning rather than just the instruction.

What is actually happening

A bottle leaves the medicine room and goes in a cowboy’s truck. Redwing charges it to the cattle at that moment. But it may be two or three weeks before any of it goes in an animal, and some of it never does — it comes back, or it sits there until the next count finds it.

At 30 September that was $2,419.56 across four drugs — 2,725 mL — sitting in trucks and saddle bags that Redwing had already expensed. That is roughly 12% of the whole medicine inventory. It is not a rounding difference and it does not wash out inside a month; it just moves to a different month and lands on different cattle.

The two ways to handle it

  Method A — charge it out when it leaves the room (what we do today) Method B — keep it in inventory until it is used
The entry One entry when the bottle leaves the medicine room. Nothing when the bottle leaves the room. One monthly journal entry for what was actually given.
What 117500 holds The medicine room only. It is understated by whatever the crew is carrying. The medicine room and the trucks. It equals the physical count.
Month end A true-up entry to add truck stock back. A different number every month, worked out by hand. None. The count is the balance.
Where the expense lands The month the bottle was issued, against whatever cattle were on the books then. The month the dose went in the animal, against the lot that got it.
Reconciling A bridge with several moving parts, like the one attached. One line: does the count equal the balance?
My recommendation is Method B, and it needs no new account and no change to the chart of accounts. It changes when the entry is made, not where it goes. The true-up we are making right now for the $2,419.56 is the transition entry — make it once and 117500 never needs it again.

Why B is both less work and more accurate

Less work. Method A needs a hand-computed true-up every single month, and its size is whatever the crew happens to be holding that day. Method B replaces that with one journal entry taken off a report the ranch program already produces: the month’s usage by medication, by lot, at the cost the product was actually bought at. There is nothing to work out.

More accurate. Under Method A, September’s expense rides on cattle that were treated in October. Cost of gain and the lot closeouts both read from animal health, so that error does not stay in one place — it moves to whichever lot happens to be open. Under Method B the cost lands on the animal that got the dose.

It is only possible now. Without a count of the trucks you could not run Method B, because the balance would include product nobody had ever verified. That is what the count bought us.

What each month looks like under B

  1. Count. The crew counts their trucks and the office counts the room, the same day, at month end. Bottles and fractions — half a bottle, three quarters — the program does the arithmetic.
  2. Clear the approvals queue first. Any treatment still waiting for approval is not in the ledger yet, and a count taken over the top of it would book those doses as shrink and then book them again when the approval posts. The program refuses to post a count while the queue has anything in it, so this is enforced rather than remembered.
  3. Post the count. Office does this in the ranch program.
  4. Take the usage report. The program gives the month’s usage by medication and by lot.
  5. One entry. Credit 117500 for the total used, debit animal health by lot.
  6. Check it. 117500 after the entry should equal the counted value. If it does not, the difference is shrink, and the program names the drug and the amount rather than leaving you to find it.

Four things to watch

  1. Shrink becomes visible, and it did not used to be. Today a bottle that goes missing out of a truck was already expensed the day it left the room, so it never shows up anywhere. Under Method B it shows as a count shortfall with a name on it. That is the point of doing this — but the crew should hear it from us before the first count, not after.
  2. The trucks have to get counted. Monthly at the least. The program handles a month where somebody does not report — it carries their last figure forward and marks the count as estimated, rather than booking shrink that did not happen. That is the exception, not the routine.
  3. We run amounts and dollars side by side for a while. Every reconciliation will carry both columns until we are satisfied the program and Redwing agree. The attached sheet shows why: of the entries on it, every one is a quantity difference times that line’s own rate — except two, and those two are the errors. Dollars alone would not have told us which was which. We can go to dollars only once we have a few clean months.
  4. Nothing accrues until we switch it on. The ranch program records no usage against inventory until a start date is set. Method B begins the day the opening count is posted, and not before.

Jake Taylor

His processing medicine is a different case and does not need the same treatment. Processing product is generally issued and used the same day, so Method A and Method B land in the same place. The ranch program carries his stock on its own and ties it to his lots, so the cost reaches the right cattle either way. Nothing for you to change there.

Nothing has been posted in the ranch program; the count is a draft until the entries on the attached reconciliation are agreed. The transition entry is on that sheet — it is the same $2,419.56, and it only ever has to be made once.
